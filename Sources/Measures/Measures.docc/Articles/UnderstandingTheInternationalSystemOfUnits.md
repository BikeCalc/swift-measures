# Understanding the International System of Units

Learn how the metric system became an international standard and how its base units, derived units, and prefixes work.

## Overview

Measurement systems provide shared definitions for expressing physical quantities. A useful system allows people to
reproduce measurements, compare results, and communicate values without ambiguity.

The International System of Units, abbreviated SI, is the internationally agreed system of measurement and the modern
form of the metric system. It provides a common measurement language for science, industry, trade, and daily life.

The SI specifies seven base units, one for each base quantity. Derived units are formed by multiplying, dividing, or
raising base units and other derived units to powers. Since 2019, all SI units have been defined using fixed values of
constants of nature, making the system stable and reproducible without relying on a particular physical artifact.

## The Metric System

The term *metric system* describes a family of decimal measurement systems that began in France during the French
Revolution. The SI is its modern international form.

Not every metric unit belongs to the SI. Historical systems such as Centimetre-Gram-Second used different coherent
units, and units such as the liter and hectare are accepted for use with the SI without being SI units themselves.

For professional and international communication, the SI should be the default. Its decimal scaling, coherent units,
and shared definitions make measurements easier to calculate, exchange, and verify across countries and disciplines.
Older systems remain necessary in some legal, cultural, and specialist contexts, but their use should be explicit.

Prefixes remove the need to learn unrelated scaling rules for each unit. Converting between prefixed units means
moving by a power of ten rather than remembering factors such as 12, 16, 20, 1760, or 5280.

### Base Units

| Dimension | Base Unit | Symbol |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Amount of substance | mole | `mol` |
| Electric current | ampere | `A` |
| Length | meter | `m` |
| Luminous intensity | candela | `cd` |
| Mass | kilogram | `kg` |
| Thermodynamic temperature | kelvin | `K` |
| Time | second | `s` |

### Derived Units

A derived unit does not need a special name. Force uses the specially named unit newton (`N`), while speed uses the
compound unit meter per second (`m/s`). Both are SI derived units.

A coherent SI unit is related to the SI base units without an additional numerical conversion factor. A coherent
derived unit is not itself an SI base unit. For example, the pascal is the coherent derived unit of pressure and is
equal to one newton per square meter.

| Dimension | Composition | Base Unit | Symbol |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Absorbed dose | energy ÷ mass | gray | `Gy` |
| Acceleration | speed ÷ time | meter per square second | `m/s²` |
| Area | length × length | square meter | `m²` |
| Catalytic activity | amount of substance ÷ time | katal | `kat` |
| Electric charge | electric current × time | coulomb | `C` |
| Electric conductance | electric current ÷ electric potential | siemens | `S` |
| Electric potential | power ÷ electric current | volt | `V` |
| Electrical capacitance | electric charge ÷ electric potential | farad | `F` |
| Electrical inductance | magnetic flux ÷ electric current | henry | `H` |
| Electrical resistance | electric potential ÷ electric current | ohm | `Ω` |
| Energy | force × length | joule | `J` |
| Equivalent dose | absorbed dose × weighting factor | sievert | `Sv` |
| Force | mass × acceleration | newton | `N` |
| Frequency | one ÷ time | hertz | `Hz` |
| Illuminance | luminous flux ÷ area | lux | `lx` |
| Luminous flux | luminous intensity × solid angle | lumen | `lm` |
| Magnetic flux | electric potential × time | weber | `Wb` |
| Magnetic flux density | magnetic flux ÷ area | tesla | `T` |
| Mass concentration | mass ÷ volume | kilogram per cubic meter | `kg/m³` |
| Molar mass | mass ÷ amount of substance | kilogram per mole | `kg/mol` |
| Power | energy ÷ time | watt | `W` |
| Pressure | force ÷ area | pascal | `Pa` |
| Radioactivity | decays ÷ time | becquerel | `Bq` |
| Speed | length ÷ time | meter per second | `m/s` |
| Surface density | mass ÷ area | kilogram per square meter | `kg/m²` |
| Volume | area × length | cubic meter | `m³` |

Different quantities can share the same dimension and coherent unit expression. For example, frequency and
radioactivity both have the dimension of reciprocal time, while absorbed dose and equivalent dose both use joules per
kilogram. Their distinct unit names preserve the meanings of the quantities they represent.

## The Imperial System

The Imperial system was standardized in Britain and used throughout much of the former British Empire. It includes
units such as inches, feet, yards, miles, ounces, pounds, stones, pints, and gallons.

The United Kingdom now requires metric units for most trade, with limited exceptions such as pints for draught beer
and cider and troy ounces for precious metals. Imperial units nevertheless remain common in road distances, personal
height, and other everyday contexts.

Imperial scaling is not uniform. A foot contains 12 inches, a yard contains 3 feet, and a mile contains 1760 yards.
This makes conversions less systematic than SI prefix conversions.

## The U.S. Customary System

The U.S. customary system is the collection of non-SI units commonly used in the United States. It developed from
earlier English systems and includes units drawn from traditions such as avoirdupois, troy, and apothecaries' measure.

U.S. customary units should not be called Imperial units. The systems share many names, but some definitions differ.
This is particularly important for volume and mass.

| Unit name | Imperial definition | U.S. customary definition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fluid ounce | approximately 28.413 mL | approximately 29.574 mL |
| Pint | approximately 568.261 mL | approximately 473.176 mL |
| Gallon | exactly 4.54609 L | exactly 3.785411784 L |
| Hundredweight | 112 lb | 100 lb |
| Ton | 2240 lb, the long ton | 2000 lb, the short ton |

A value labeled only `gallon`, `pint`, or `ton` is incomplete when its measurement system is not already unambiguous.
Software APIs should use explicit names such as `imperialGallon` and `usLiquidGallon`.

## Other Historical Systems

SI, Imperial, and U.S. customary units are not the only systems that have existed. Before metrication, France used
numerous measures whose definitions could vary by region and trade. Other societies developed their own systems for
length, land, volume, and mass, including Roman, Chinese, Japanese, Indian, Ottoman, and Russian traditions.

Many older units have since been replaced in official and international work, while some survive in cultural,
historical, legal, or specialized contexts. Their existence is a reminder that a familiar unit is not automatically a
universal one.

The creation of the metric system was itself a response to fragmented local measurement. The Metre Convention of 1875
then established a permanent international framework for improving and unifying measurement standards.
